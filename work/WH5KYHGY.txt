Predictability-Aware Subsequence Modeling for Sequential Recommendation
Hangyu Deng, Non-member
Jinglu Hua, Member
Sequential recommendation frames the recommendation task as a next-item prediction problem, where the model is trained
to predict the next item given a user behavior sequence. While recent research has made signiﬁcant progress in developing
advanced models for this task, there exists a notable gap in the exploration of subsequences and the predictability inherent
in user behavior sequences. This oversight can lead models to recall inconsequential sequential patterns, adversely affecting
recommendation quality. In this paper, we introduce a novel approach to augmenting sequential recommendation by integrating
predictability awareness into subsequence modeling. Our method begins by discerning the predictability of target items; those
easily predicted often align with the preceding subsequence, while those that are hard to predict typically indicate transitions
to other subsequences. Leveraging this predictability information, we enhance the discovery of meaningful subsequences within
individual user behavior sequences. Evaluation of four benchmark data sets using various state-of-the-art sequential models
illustrates the efﬁcacy of our approach in enhancing recommendation performance. © 2024 Institute of Electrical Engineers of
Japan and Wiley Periodicals LLC.

1.
Introduction
In the digital age, where information overload is a common
challenge, recommender systems play a pivotal role in shaping
user experiences and facilitating content discovery [1,2]. These
systems have become indispensable in various online platforms,
ranging from e-commerce to social media. By analyzing user pref-
erences and behaviors, recommender systems intelligently suggest
personalized items, whether products, movies, or content, thereby
enhancing user satisfaction and engagement. The ability to provide
tailored recommendations not only streamlines decision-making
processes for users but also contributes to increased user retention
and business revenue [3]. As the demand for personalized
experiences continues to grow, the importance of advancing
recommender
system
methodologies
becomes
increasingly
pronounced.
The primary objective of recommender systems is to model
how users interact with items. Sequential recommendation, which
takes into account the chronological order of interactions, has been
shown to be effective in capturing user preferences over time.
Speciﬁcally, it formulates the recommendation task as a next-item
prediction problem, aiming to predict the item that a user is most
likely to visit next. However, mining meaningful patterns from
sequential data remains a challenging task. Despite the wealth of
personalized information that can be mined from user behavior
sequences, extracting interesting and meaningful patterns from this
type of data is still an open research question.

To better encode the behavior sequences, recent studies have
turned to deep sequence models, including recurrent neural
networks (RNNs) [4] and Transformers [5]. Notably, autoregres-
sive training, a technique widely successful in natural language
processing (NLP), is commonly employed. In general, it is
reasonable to employ methods in the NLP ﬁeld for sequential
recommendation since the task bears similarities to language
modeling.
Although sequential recommendation models have exhibited
remarkable performance, it is crucial to acknowledge the dissim-
ilarities between user behavior data and textual data. Generally
speaking, user behavior sequences tend to be more diverse and
unpredictable compared to natural language sentences. After all,
a sentence needs to comply with grammar rules and adhere to the
logic of the context, while user behavior does not have such strict
constraints. For example, Fig. 1 shows a fragment of a behavior
sequence in the training set of the Amazon_Electronics data set.
The user behavior sequence is a diverse mixture of items spanning
various categories. Multiple subsequences emerge, each associated
with distinct themes such as MP3s, laptops, and cameras. This
suggests a broad interest by the user across different types of
items over time. However, the autoregressive training mechanism
employed by current sequential recommenders [6–9] ignores the
differences between items and treats them equally. It calculates
the losses between target items and model predictions and updates
the parameters, leading the model to remember and predict the
next items, including those that are not related, even if they do
not follow any discernible pattern. This training process can result
in the model falsely considering a connection to unpredictable
items as a reasonable pattern.
In this paper, we aim to model the subsequences of user behav-
ior sequences by incorporating predictability information. A novel

relevance of recommended items to individual users. Besides, our
approach can be integrated into existing recommendation systems,
leveraging their infrastructure and data pipelines. It can serve as
a ranking module directly. In addition, it can be implemented as
a pre-processing step in the recommendation pipeline, where the
predictability of items is assessed before feeding the data into the
recommendation model. By analyzing the predictability of items
in users’ browsing histories, the system can deliver more relevant
product recommendations, leading to increased user satisfaction
and higher conversion rates.
The contributions are summarized as follows:
• We illuminate the signiﬁcance of incorporating predictability
considerations into user behavior data for the sequential
recommendation, which explicitly reduces the risk of the
model memorizing inconsequential patterns.
• We address the challenge posed by hard items in sequential
recommendation through a practical subsequence modeling
approach, providing a systematic method to enhance the
model’s adaptability to dynamic user preferences.
• We conduct extensive experiments on four widely-used
public data sets, which verify the effectiveness of the
proposed method.
2.
Related Work
Sequential recommendation methods have evolved into a spe-
cialized domain within recommender systems, designed to capture
the temporal dynamics and evolving preferences of users. These
approaches consider the sequential nature of user-item interactions,
leveraging dependencies between past and future actions. FPMC
[10] operates on the assumption that the behavior sequence forms
a Markov chain conditioned on the user. GRU4Rec [6] employs
an RNN-based architecture to encode item sequences, utilizing a
sequence-parallel mini-batch strategy for training. SASRec [7] uti-
lizes self-attention mechanisms to capture long-term dependencies,
demonstrating notable performance in sequential recommendation
tasks. BERT4Rec [11] draws inspiration from BERT [12], imple-
menting a bidirectional Transformer model and adapting BERT-
style training strategies for sequential recommendation through
the introduction of the Cloze objective. LightSANs [8] enhances
SASRec by introducing a low-rank decomposed self-attention
mechanism, reducing computational complexity while preserving
performance.
In the context of modeling users’ multiple interests, recent
methods typically introduce a set of user representation vec-
tors. MIND [13] introduces a multi-interest framework with
a dynamic routing algorithm [14] to extract multiple inter-
ests from user behaviors. SINE [15] employs a sparse multi-
interest module to identify a speciﬁc set of concepts for each
user, narrowing down the range of interests and enabling more
focused and personalized recommendations. GSSE4Rec [16]
utilizes global item transitions to improve the extraction of
subsequences.

The overview of our method is depicted in Fig. 2, consisting

3.1.
Assessing the predictability of target items
Due to the high-dimensional, sparse, and complex nature of data
in sequential recommendation, it is challenging to identify the
predictability of target items directly. Our approach to circumvent
this issue is to employ a fast and well-established model. First,
let us consider a normal case. If a target item in the test set can
be correctly predicted by the model, we can conﬁdently say it
is predictable. If not, it may be hard to predict or just inherently
unpredictable. The goal is to assess the predictability of target
items in the training set, but it is inadequate to use a model
trained on the original training set to make this determination.
To overcome this limitation, we can employ the leave-one-out
strategy, where we leave one data sample out of the training set
and train a model on the remaining data. We then test the left-out
sample to obtain a ﬂag indicating whether the target item is
predictable according to the test result. To avoid data leakage, we
need to ensure that the smallest unit left out is a user with all the
corresponding samples. Although such a leave-one-out strategy
helps us obtain a model very close to the model trained on the orig-
inal data, it is unrealistic to train a brand new model for evaluating
the predictability of target items for each user. So we approximate
this process by employing a similar idea to k-fold cross-validation.
Think of this process as testing whether a model can accurately
predict what a user will do next based on their past behavior.
We train the model on most of the data but leave out a small por-
tion to see how well it predicts the next action. By repeating this
process for different users and items, we can assess how pre-
dictable certain items are in a way that is both accurate and
efﬁcient.

Model structure
Figure 3 illustrates the architecture
of the proposed neural network model. The model takes as
input the raw user behavior sequence and encodes them using
a Transformer encoder. Next, a subsequence assignment (SA)

the subsequences are processed by a series of sequence models
tailored for handling different user intentions. Finally, in the output
layer, the ﬁnal user representation is generated by merging the
results from the sequence models.

3.4.2. Broader implications of predictability-aware mod-
eling
The application of predictability-aware modeling extends
beyond sequential recommendation to various domains of machine
learning and data analysis. In time-series forecasting, such models
can enhance the accuracy of predictions by incorporating informa-
tion about the predictability of data points. Similarly, in anomaly
detection tasks, considering the predictability of data can improve
the identiﬁcation of abnormal patterns and reduce false positives.
3.4.3. Potential limitations and future research directions
As with any modeling approach, there exist potential limitations
and avenues for future research that warrant consideration. One
limitation is the reliance on predeﬁned predictability thresholds,
which may not always capture the nuanced predictability patterns
present in diverse data sets. Future research could explore adaptive

4.
Experiments
In this section, we outline our experimental methodology
designed to assess the efﬁcacy of our proposed approach. First, we
provide a comprehensive introduction to the data sets, evaluation
metrics, and competing methods. Next, we conduct a comparative
analysis of our proposed method and various competitors. Finally,
we empirically validate the key designs of our approach.
4.1.
Experimental settings
4.1.1. Data sets
Our proposed methods undergo evaluation
on four extensively utilized real-world data sets derived from
two primary sources: Amazon [18] and MovieLens [19]. The
Amazon data set, widely employed in previous studies [7,8,17],
encompasses user reviews across diverse items within the Amazon
platform. It stands out as a relatively sparse data set tailored for
sequential recommendation. For our assessment, we opt for three
speciﬁc subsets, namely Beauty, Video Games, and Electronics.
The Movielens data set stands as a well-established benchmark,
housing user ratings for a myriad of movies. In our experiments,
we leverage the Movielens-1M (ML-1M) data set, known for its
density and widespread use [7–9]. This data set exhibits relatively
dense user-item interactions, featuring a moderate number of users
and items but comprising a substantial one million interactions.
To ensure a robust data set, we enforce the 5-core setting,
ﬁltering the original data to guarantee each user has a minimum
of ﬁve behaviors, and each item is interacted with by at least ﬁve
users within the remaining data set, which is a common practice in

4.1.2. Evaluation metrics
In our experimental evaluation,
we employ two widely recognized metrics: Hit Ratio (HIT) and
Normalized Discounted Cumulative Gain (NDCG). HIT@k is
deﬁned as the proportion of users for whom the system accurately
places the target item within the top k recommendations [20].
NDCG@k incorporates the positional information of items within
the list of the top k recommendations [21]. This metric operates on
the premise that items ranked higher should receive greater credit
than their lower-ranked counterparts.
To eliminate potential biases, we employ a rigorous evaluation
approach where the ground-truth item is ranked alongside all other
items [22].

4.1.3. Competitors
In our experimental evaluations, we
assess the performance of our proposed method against several
representative and state-of-the-art sequential recommendation
models. These models fall into two distinct categories, with the
ﬁrst group comprising techniques that predominantly rely on
sequence modeling:
• FPMC [10]: This method adheres to the Markov chain
assumption for user behavior sequences. It integrates both
user-item interactions and ﬁrst-order item-item dynamics.
• GRU4Rec [6]: This method applies Gated Recurrent Unit
(GRU) [23] architecture for modeling sequential patterns.
• SASRec [7]: This method encodes user behavior sequences
with Transformer encoders [5], capitalizing on self-attention
mechanisms to capture sequential information.
• LightSANs [8]: This method is a recent state-of-the-
art model derived from SASRec. LightSANs introduces
a low-rank self-attention mechanism to mitigate over-
parameterization within the full self-attention matrix.
The second group encompasses models designed to capture
multiple interests of the user:
• MIND [13]: It proposes a dynamic routing algorithm for
modeling multi-interest recommendations.
• SINE [15]: It introduces a sparse-interest module. SINE
adaptively infers a sparse set of concepts for each user.
• GSSE4SR [16]: It leverages global item transitions to
enhance the extraction of subsequences.
4.1.4. Implementation details
We implement our method
on Recbole [24] (Version 1.1.1), a comprehensive platform for
recommendation research. Additionally, for competitor models, we
utilize the pre-existing implementations provided by Recbole.
In the assessment of predictability, we employ SASRec [7]
model as the fFastModel, which is known for its efﬁciency and
accuracy. The number of subsets K is set to 5 to ensure the
performance of detection. The portion of target items to be
considered predictable (T) is selected from 0.1 to 0.9, and the
hyperparameter p that controls the weight of hard-to-predict items
is selected from 0.0 to 0.9, w.r.t. the data set.
For the network structure, we maintain an embedding size d of
64, following the default setting in Recbole. The maximum length
n is set to 50 for Amazon data sets and 200 for ML-1 M data set,
consistent with established practices in related works [7,11]. We
consider dropout rates from {0.2,0.5}. The number of subsequences
c is chosen from 1 to 5.

4.3.
Analysis of hyperparameters
Here we assess
the impact of hyperparameters by systematically varying one
parameter at a time while keeping others constant. The results
are visually presented in Fig. 4.
Concerning subsequence modeling, the key hyperparameter is
the number of subsequences c inﬂuencing the model’s granular-
ity in understanding user behavior. We measure the performance
using representative metrics, HIT@10 and NDCG@10, which are
then scaled into the [0.0,1.0] interval for clarity. Our observa-
tions reveal distinct optimal values for different data sets. For
the Amazon data set, the best performance emerges with two
subsequences, indicating that a more detailed analysis of user
behavior, achieved through two segments, enhances recommen-
dation outcomes. Conversely, the Movielens data set achieves
superior performance with four local subsequences. This suggests
that the Movielens data set beneﬁts from a broader exploration of
user behavior, with four segments providing a more informative
representation. The discrepancy in the optimal number of local
subsequences can be attributed to the diverse characteristics of the
data sets. The Amazon data set, characterized by sparsity, beneﬁts
from a focused analysis with two local subsequences. In con-
trast, the Movielens data set, featuring more interactions per user,
necessitates a broader perspective and a higher number of local
subsequences to effectively capture intricate patterns and diverse
user interests.

4.4.
Ablation study
In our ablation study, we systemati-
cally remove key components from our proposed method to discern
their individual contributions and assess their impact on overall
performance. The HIT@10 performances of the proposed method,
with each component omitted, are documented in Table III. Here,
“w/o WL” denotes the proposed method without the weighted loss.
“w/o LOS” signiﬁes the proposed method without the leave-out
strategy. It means that during the assessment of predictability, the
model is trained on the whole training set directly and tested on the
same training set to detect the predictability of items. Our ﬁndings
reveal a noticeable decrease in performance when any one of these
key components is removed. Notably, the weighted loss emerges
as a crucial factor, as its absence leads to a signiﬁcant decline in
performance.
5.
Conclusions
This paper introduces a novel approach to sequential recom-
mendation systems, aiming to enhance subsequence modeling
by leveraging the predictability of items. Our proposed method
employs a leave-out strategy to evaluate the predictability of items
in the training set. The obtained predictability information is then
utilized to identify subsequence boundaries and adjust the loss
functions for items that are challenging or impossible to predict.
Additionally, our approach incorporates a module designed to
discover relevant subsequences for individual users. This enables
downstream sequence models to concentrate on meaningful item
transitions while disregarding irrelevant ones. We thoroughly
evaluate the effectiveness of our method across four public data
sets, consistently outperforming existing alternatives and deliv-
ering competitive results. Furthermore, we conduct an in-depth
analysis of the key components of our model to validate their
efﬁcacy and assess their impact on recommendation performance.
